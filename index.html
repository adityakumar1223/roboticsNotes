Robotics — The Basics

Start here! Learn what robotics is, its laws, types, and components.

📖 1. What is Robotics?

Robotics is the branch of technology that deals with the design, construction, operation, and use of robots. It combines several fields including mechanical engineering, electrical engineering, computer science, and artificial intelligence.

Simple Definition: Robotics is the science and technology of building machines (robots) that can perform tasks automatically — either by following fixed instructions or by sensing the world around them and making decisions.

What is a Robot?

A robot is a programmable mechanical machine that can perform tasks with speed, precision, and consistency — often in environments that are dangerous, repetitive, or impossible for humans.

🏭

Industrial Robots

Used in factories for welding, painting, assembly

🏠

Service Robots

Used in homes — like Roomba vacuum cleaner

🚀

Space Robots

Mars Rovers exploring other planets

💊

Medical Robots

Da Vinci surgical robot performs precise surgery

🪖

Military Robots

Bomb disposal robots, drones

🎓

Educational Robots

LEGO Mindstorms, Arduino-based bots

Key Areas of Robotics

  • Mechanical Design – How the robot's body is built (arms, wheels, grippers)
  • Electronics – Circuits, sensors, actuators, power systems
  • Programming – Code that tells the robot what to do
  • Artificial Intelligence – Letting robots learn and make decisions
  • Control Systems – How the robot monitors and adjusts its own behavior

⚖️ 2. The Three Laws of Robotics

These famous laws were written by science-fiction author Isaac Asimov in 1942. They are a set of rules that fictional robots must follow — and they still influence real-world robot ethics today.

🔴 First Law

"A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm."

🟡 Second Law

"A robot must obey the orders given to it by human beings, except where such orders would conflict with the First Law."

🟢 Third Law

"A robot must protect its own existence as long as such protection does not conflict with the First or Second Law."

📌 Remember: The laws are ordered by priority. The First Law beats the Second, and the Second beats the Third. Asimov later added a "Zeroth Law": A robot may not harm humanity as a whole.

🦴 4. Robot Anatomy

Robot Anatomy refers to the physical structure and internal organization of a robot — just like human anatomy studies the human body.

Figure: Basic Anatomy of a Robot showing its key components

Main Parts of a Robot

  • Sensors – Detect the environment (cameras, ultrasonic, IR, temperature)
  • Actuators – Create movement (motors, servos, pneumatics)
  • Controller (Brain) – Processes data and gives commands (microcontroller, computer)
  • Power Supply – Provides energy (battery, solar, wired)
  • Links – Rigid structural parts connecting joints
  • Joints – Connecting points that allow movement between links

🔄 9. Pitch, Yaw, and Roll

These are the three fundamental rotations describing how an object (airplane, drone, robot) can move in 3D space. Think of an airplane to understand them easily.

Figure: Pitch, Yaw, Roll — the three rotational axes

🔴 Pitch

Rotation around the left-right (lateral) axis. Like nodding your head YES. Makes the nose go up or down.

🟢 Yaw

Rotation around the vertical (up-down) axis. Like shaking your head NO. Turns left or right.

🟡 Roll

Rotation around the front-back (longitudinal) axis. Like tilting your head to the side. Wings tilt.

📐 10. Degrees of Freedom (DOF)

Degrees of Freedom (DOF) describes the number of independent movements a robot (or any mechanical system) can make. The more DOF, the more flexible and versatile the robot.

Think of it this way: A door can only open/close → 1 DOF. Your shoulder can move in many directions → 3 DOF. A full human arm has about 7 DOF!

Types of DOF

  • Translational DOF – Linear movement (forward/back, up/down, left/right) along X, Y, Z axes
  • Rotational DOF – Rotation around axes (Pitch, Yaw, Roll)

In 3D Space

An object in free 3D space has 6 DOF: 3 translational (X, Y, Z) + 3 rotational (Pitch, Yaw, Roll). This is why many robot arms are designed with 6 joints.

Examples

Robot/ObjectDOF
A door hinge1
A 2D drawing robot2
SCARA robot arm4
Standard industrial arm6
Human arm (shoulder to wrist)7
Full humanoid body30+
Significance: More DOF = more flexibility but also more complexity in programming and control. A 6-DOF robot arm can position its end effector at any point in 3D space with any orientation.

🦾 6. Robot Manipulator

A robot manipulator is a mechanical arm system — the "arm" of a robot — designed to manipulate objects in its environment. It mimics the function of a human arm and hand.

Structure of a Manipulator

  • Base – Fixed mounting point (attached to floor or surface)
  • Links – Rigid arm segments (like bones in your arm)
  • Joints – Connect links and allow relative motion (like elbows, wrists)
  • End Effector – The "hand" — a gripper, tool, or welding torch
Real-World Example: The robotic arm in a car factory uses a manipulator with 6 joints to weld car body parts. Each joint can rotate, giving it complete freedom to reach any position in its workspace.

Types of Manipulators

  • Cartesian – Moves in straight X,Y,Z lines (like a 3D printer head)
  • Cylindrical – One rotary + two linear joints
  • Spherical/Polar – Two rotary + one linear joint
  • Articulated – All rotary joints, most human-like and flexible
  • SCARA – Horizontal rotary joints, great for assembly tasks

⚡ 7. What are Actuators?

An actuator is a device that converts energy into physical motion. It's what makes robots move! The controller sends a signal, and the actuator performs the physical action.

⚡

Electric Actuators

DC motors, servo motors, stepper motors. Most common in robotics. Easy to control digitally.

💨

Pneumatic Actuators

Powered by compressed air. Very fast and powerful. Used in industrial arms.

💧

Hydraulic Actuators

Powered by pressurized fluid. Extremely high force. Used in heavy machinery.

Most Common Actuators in Student Robots

  • DC Motor – Rotates continuously, used for wheels. Speed controlled by PWM.
  • Servo Motor – Rotates to a specific angle (0°–180°). Used for robot arms/joints.
  • Stepper Motor – Moves in precise small steps. Used in 3D printers, CNC machines.
  • Linear Actuator – Converts rotation to straight-line push/pull movement.

Control Systems in Robotics

Learn how robots manage, monitor, and correct their own movements.

🎛️ What is a Control System?

A control system is a set of devices and algorithms that manages, commands, directs, or regulates the behavior of another system (the robot) to achieve a desired outcome.

Everyday Analogy: When you drive a car, your eyes (sensors) see the road, your brain (controller) decides what to do, and your hands/feet (actuators) steer and brake. If you drift too far right, you correct it. That correction is what a control system does — automatically.

Basic Control System Diagram

Figure: Basic Open-Loop Control System Flow

➡️ Non-Servo (Open Loop) Control System

In an open-loop (non-servo) control system, the controller sends a command to the actuator, and the system performs the action — but it does NOT check whether the desired result was achieved. There is no feedback.

Examples of Open Loop Systems

  • 🚿 Water sprinkler – Runs for a fixed time regardless of how wet the lawn is
  • 🍞 Toaster – Toasts for a set time, doesn't check if bread is done
  • ⏱️ Washing machine (timer-based) – Runs for preset time, not based on cleanliness
  • 🤖 Simple robot moving for 2 seconds – Doesn't know if it actually traveled the distance
Limitation: Open-loop systems can fail if conditions change (e.g., motor slows due to load) because there's no way to detect and correct errors.

🔁 Servo (Closed Loop) Control System

In a closed-loop (servo) control system, the output is continuously measured and fed back to the controller. If there's a difference between the desired state and actual state (called the error), the controller corrects it automatically.

Figure: Closed-Loop (Servo) Control System with Feedback

Examples of Closed Loop Systems

  • 🌡️ Air conditioner thermostat – Measures room temperature, turns on/off to maintain set temp
  • 🚗 Car cruise control – Measures actual speed, adjusts engine to maintain target speed
  • 🤖 Servo motor in robot arm – Encoder measures actual angle, corrects if off target
  • 🎮 Line-following robot – IR sensors detect line position, adjusts motor speeds to stay on line
✅ Advantage: Closed-loop systems are much more accurate and robust. They automatically correct errors caused by disturbances, load changes, or component variations.

Role of Closed Loop in Robotics

In robotics, closed-loop control is essential for:

  • Precise positioning of robot arms (within millimeter accuracy)
  • Maintaining balance in bipedal/humanoid robots
  • Speed regulation of motors under varying loads
  • Following a path accurately (GPS-based navigation)
  • Grasping objects with the right force (force sensors + feedback)

✅ Characteristics of a Good Control System

  • Accuracy – The output matches the desired value precisely
  • Stability – The system settles to steady state without oscillating wildly
  • Speed of Response – Quickly reaches the desired output
  • Sensitivity – Low sensitivity to disturbances and parameter changes
  • Robustness – Works correctly even when conditions change
  • Noise Rejection – Filters out unwanted signals
  • Reliability – Works consistently over time without failures
  • Simplicity – Easier to design, maintain, and troubleshoot

🔧 Torque, Moment of Inertia, and Power

Torque (τ)

Torque is a measure of the rotational force applied to an object. It determines how much twisting/rotational effect a force produces.

// Formula:
Torque (τ) = Force (F) × Distance (r)
// Units: Newton-meters (N·m)

// Example: Opening a door
F = 10 N, r = 0.5 m (distance from hinge)
τ = 10 × 0.5 = 5 N·m

In robotics, a motor's torque determines how much weight/load a robot arm can lift. Higher torque = can move heavier loads.

Moment of Inertia (I)

Moment of Inertia is the resistance of an object to changes in its rotational motion. A heavier or larger object has more inertia — it's harder to start spinning and harder to stop.

// Formula for a simple point mass:
I = m × r²
// m = mass (kg), r = distance from axis (m)
// Units: kg·m²

Analogy: A figure skater spins faster when they pull their arms in (reduce r) — reducing moment of inertia.

Power (P)

Power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.

// Formula:
P = Work / Time OR P = Force × Velocity
// For rotation: P = Torque × Angular velocity (ω)
// Units: Watts (W)

In robotics, choosing the right motor means balancing torque, speed (RPM), and power consumption.

Robot Anatomy — Links, Joints & DOF

How a robot's mechanical body is structured and how it moves.

🔗 Links in Robotic Systems

Links are the rigid structural members of a robot arm — they are the "bones" of the robot. Links do not move by themselves; they transmit force and motion from one joint to another.

Types of Links

  • Base Link – Fixed to the ground or mounting surface. Acts as reference frame.
  • Intermediate Links – Connect joints in sequence (like upper arm, forearm in human)
  • End Effector Link – The last link, holding the tool or gripper
Analogy: Think of links as the bones in your arm:
Upper arm = Link 1, Forearm = Link 2, Hand = End Effector
Shoulder, Elbow, Wrist = Joints connecting these links

Role of Links

  • Provide structural strength and rigidity to the robot arm
  • Define the workspace (reach) of the robot
  • Transmit motion from motors/actuators to end effector
  • Longer links → larger workspace but less precision and more bending stress

🔩 Joints in Robotics

Joints are the connections between links that allow relative motion. Each joint provides one or more degrees of freedom. The type of joint determines what kind of motion is possible.

🔄 Revolute Joint (R)

Also called a rotary joint. It allows rotation around a single axis — like a hinge or door knob. Most common joint in robot arms.

  • 1 DOF – only rotational
  • Example: elbow joint, shoulder rotation
  • Used in: all articulated robot arms

↔️ Prismatic Joint (P)

Also called a sliding/linear joint. It allows movement along a single axis — like a piston or drawer. Extends or retracts linearly.

  • 1 DOF – only translational
  • Example: hydraulic cylinder, telescoping arm
  • Used in: pick-and-place systems, CNC machines

Other Joint Types

Joint TypeMotionDOFExample
Revolute (R)Rotation around one axis1Elbow, door hinge
Prismatic (P)Linear sliding motion1Piston, drawer
Cylindrical (C)Rotation + Translation on same axis2Drill press chuck

Role of Joints: Joints give the robot its ability to move and reach different positions. The number and type of joints directly determines the robot's DOF, workspace, and capabilities.

Sensors in Robotics

How robots perceive the world — from light to temperature to touch.

🔍 What are Sensors?

A sensor is a device that detects changes in the environment and converts them into signals (usually electrical) that a microcontroller or computer can process. Sensors are the "eyes, ears, and skin" of a robot.

Active Sensors

Active sensors emit energy (light, sound, radio waves) and detect the reflected signal. They need a power source to operate.

  • Ultrasonic sensor (emits sound waves)
  • IR sensor (emits infrared light)
  • LIDAR (emits laser pulses)
  • Radar (emits radio waves)

Passive Sensors

Passive sensors only detect energy from the environment. They don't emit anything.

  • Camera (captures ambient light)
  • LDR (measures existing light level)
  • Thermocouple (measures temperature)
  • Microphone (detects sound from environment)

Tactile vs Non-Tactile Sensors

FeatureTactile SensorsNon-Tactile Sensors
Contact needed?Yes — must touch objectNo — works at a distance
ExamplesForce sensor, touch sensor, pressure padIR, ultrasonic, camera, LIDAR
Use caseGrasping objects, detecting collisionNavigation, obstacle avoidance
RangeVery short (direct contact)Short to long range

👆 Tactile Sensor

A tactile sensor is a sensor that detects physical contact, pressure, or force — mimicking the sense of touch in humans. It senses when something touches the robot and how hard it is pressing.

How It Works

  • When pressure is applied, the sensor material deforms slightly
  • This deformation changes an electrical property (resistance, capacitance, or voltage)
  • The change is measured and converted to a pressure/force value

Types of Tactile Sensors

  • Force/Torque Sensor – Measures the magnitude and direction of force
  • Pressure Sensor Array – Grid of pressure points (like artificial skin)
  • Limit Switch – Simple on/off touch detection
  • Piezoelectric Sensor – Generates voltage when pressed
Application in Robotics: Used in robot grippers to control grasping force — the robot can pick up a fragile egg without crushing it by monitoring tactile feedback.

📐 TILT Sensor

A tilt sensor detects the orientation or angle of an object relative to gravity. It senses whether something is tilted beyond a certain angle.

Working Principle

The most common basic tilt sensor uses a mercury ball or metal ball inside a cylinder:

1

Level Position

The metal ball rests at the bottom of the cylinder, completing an electrical circuit → Output: HIGH (1)

2

Tilted Position

When tilted beyond threshold angle, the ball rolls away → Circuit breaks → Output: LOW (0)

3

Signal Sent

The microcontroller reads this digital HIGH/LOW signal to know if the device is tilted.

Advanced Tilt Sensors

  • MEMS Accelerometer (MPU-6050) – Measures acceleration in X,Y,Z axes. Can calculate precise tilt angle.
  • Gyroscope – Measures angular rate of rotation
  • IMU (Inertial Measurement Unit) – Combines accelerometer + gyroscope

Applications

  • Self-balancing robots (like Segway-style bots)
  • Drone stabilization
  • Game controllers (sensing phone tilt)
  • Alarm systems (detecting if something is moved)

💡 LDR Sensor (Light Dependent Resistor)

An LDR (also called photoresistor) is a sensor whose resistance changes based on the amount of light falling on it.

Key Principle:
🌞 More Light → Lower Resistance → Higher current flow → HIGH voltage reading
🌑 Less Light → Higher Resistance → Lower current flow → LOW voltage reading

Working Principle

LDR is made of a light-sensitive semiconductor material (usually cadmium sulfide). When photons (light particles) hit the material, they excite electrons, making more charge carriers available, reducing resistance. It's used in a voltage divider circuit to give an analog reading.

// Arduino example - LDR on analog pin A0
int ldrPin = A0;
int ldrValue = 0;

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(9600);
}

void loop() {
  ldrValue = analogRead(ldrPin); // 0-1023
  if (ldrValue < 500) {
    Serial.println("Dark!"); // Turn on LED
  } else {
    Serial.println("Bright!");
  }
}

Applications

  • Automatic street lights (turn on when dark)
  • Light-following robots
  • Camera automatic exposure control

🔴 IR Sensor (Infrared Sensor)

An IR sensor detects infrared radiation (light just below visible spectrum). It consists of two parts: an IR LED (emitter) and a photodiode (receiver).

Figure: IR Sensor Working Principle

Working Principle

  1. IR LED continuously emits infrared light
  2. If an object is nearby, IR light reflects off it
  3. The photodiode detects the reflected IR
  4. Output pin goes LOW (object detected) or HIGH (no object)

Applications

  • Line-following robots – Detects black/white line on floor (black absorbs IR, white reflects)
  • Obstacle avoidance – Detects nearby obstacles
  • TV remotes – IR sends commands to TV
  • Object counting on conveyor belts

📶 Proximity Sensor

A proximity sensor detects the presence of objects without physical contact — at close range. It senses when an object is "near" but doesn't measure exact distance.

Types of Proximity Sensors

  • Inductive Proximity Sensor – Detects metal objects using electromagnetic field (like detecting a metal key near the sensor)
  • Capacitive Proximity Sensor – Detects any object (metal or non-metal) by measuring capacitance change
  • Magnetic Proximity Sensor (Reed Switch) – Detects magnets
  • Ultrasonic Proximity Sensor – Uses sound waves, works for any material
  • Photoelectric (IR/Optical) – Uses light beam interruption
Application: Smartphones use capacitive proximity sensors to turn off the screen when you hold the phone to your ear — saving battery and preventing accidental touches.

🔊 Ultrasonic Sensor (HC-SR04)

An ultrasonic sensor measures distance by sending out high-frequency sound pulses and measuring how long they take to bounce back.

Figure: Ultrasonic sensor measuring distance using sound waves

Working Steps

1

TRIG pulse sent

Microcontroller sends a 10µs HIGH pulse to TRIG pin

2

Ultrasonic burst emitted

Sensor sends 8 cycles of 40kHz ultrasonic sound

3

ECHO goes HIGH

ECHO pin becomes HIGH immediately after burst is sent

4

Sound returns

Sound bounces off object and returns to sensor

5

Calculate distance

Distance = (ECHO pulse duration × 34300 cm/s) ÷ 2

// Arduino - Ultrasonic Distance Measurement
int trigPin = 9, echoPin = 10;
long duration; float distance;

void setup() { Serial.begin(9600); }

void loop() {
  digitalWrite(trigPin, HIGH);
  delayMicroseconds(10);
  digitalWrite(trigPin, LOW);
  duration = pulseIn(echoPin, HIGH);
  distance = duration * 0.034 / 2; // in cm
  Serial.println(distance);
}

🌡️ DHT11 Temperature & Humidity Sensor

The DHT11 is a basic, low-cost digital sensor that measures both temperature and relative humidity. It provides calibrated digital output using a single data wire.

Specifications

PropertyValue
Temperature Range0°C to 50°C
Humidity Range20% to 80% RH
Accuracy (Temp)±2°C
Accuracy (Humidity)±5% RH
Sampling Rate1 reading per second
Voltage3.3V – 5.5V
PinsVCC, Data, GND

How DHT11 Works

DHT11 uses two internal sensors:

  • Humidity sensing element – A moisture-absorbing substrate between two electrodes. Humidity changes its capacitance.
  • Thermistor (NTC) – Its resistance changes with temperature.

An internal microchip converts these readings to digital data and sends it via a proprietary single-wire protocol.

Data Format

Sends 40 bits total: 8 bits humidity integer + 8 bits humidity decimal + 8 bits temp integer + 8 bits temp decimal + 8 bits checksum

// Arduino DHT11 Example (using DHT library)
#include <DHT.h>
DHT dht(2, DHT11); // Data pin = 2

void setup() { Serial.begin(9600); dht.begin(); }

void loop() {
  float temp = dht.readTemperature();
  float hum = dht.readHumidity();
  Serial.print("Temp: "); Serial.println(temp);
  Serial.print("Humidity: "); Serial.println(hum);
  delay(1000);
}

Temperature Sensor Working in General

Temperature sensors detect heat by measuring a physical property that changes with temperature:

  • Thermistor (NTC/PTC) – Resistance changes with temperature. NTC: resistance decreases as temperature increases.
  • Thermocouple (Type K) – Two different metals joined; temperature difference generates a small voltage (Seebeck effect)
  • DS18B20 – Digital temperature sensor, very precise, uses 1-Wire protocol
  • LM35 – Analog sensor; outputs 10mV per °C directly readable by ADC
Two Common Examples:
① LM35 – Simple analog sensor, output = 10mV/°C, range: -55°C to 150°C
② DS18B20 – Digital sensor, ±0.5°C accuracy, waterproof version available for liquid temp measurement

📡 Bluetooth, GPS & GSM in Robotic Communication

Bluetooth is a short-range wireless communication technology operating at 2.4 GHz.

Microprocessors & Microcontrollers

The brain of every robot — understanding how they work.

🧠 What is a Microprocessor?

A microprocessor is an integrated circuit (IC) that contains only the Central Processing Unit (CPU) on a single chip. It performs arithmetic, logic, and control operations but requires external memory (RAM, ROM) and I/O components to function.

Simple Analogy: A microprocessor is like just the brain — it's powerful and fast, but it needs a body (memory, storage, I/O circuits) to actually do things.

Examples of Microprocessors

  • Intel 8085, 8086 (historic, used in education)
  • ARM Cortex-A series (used in smartphones)
  • Intel Core i5/i7 (used in computers)
  • Raspberry Pi's Broadcom BCM2837 (used in robotics education)

📟 What is a Microcontroller?

A microcontroller is a complete computer on a single chip. It contains a CPU + RAM + ROM/Flash + I/O ports + timers + communication interfaces — everything built-in.

Simple Analogy: A microcontroller is like a brain PLUS body — all in one small chip. It's self-contained and ready to use with minimal external components.

🔢 8051 Microcontroller — Architecture & Details

The 8051 is a classic 8-bit microcontroller developed by Intel in 1980. It is still widely used for learning and in many embedded applications.

Key Features

  • 8-bit CPU
  • 4KB internal ROM (program memory)
  • 128 bytes internal RAM
  • 32 I/O pins (4 ports × 8 bits)
  • 2 x 16-bit Timers (Timer 0 and Timer 1)
  • 1 UART serial port
  • 6 interrupt sources
  • Clock speed: typically 12 MHz

Architecture Components

  • ALU – Arithmetic Logic Unit (does math and logic)
  • Accumulator (A) – Main 8-bit register for calculations
  • B Register – Used for multiply/divide operations
  • PC (Program Counter) – Holds address of next instruction
  • SP (Stack Pointer) – Points to top of stack
  • PSW – Program Status Word (flags: carry, zero, parity, etc.)
  • DPTR – 16-bit Data Pointer for external memory access

Pin Configuration (40-pin DIP)

  • Port 0 (P0.0–P0.7) – Pins 32–39: Multiplexed I/O and Address/Data bus
  • Port 1 (P1.0–P1.7) – Pins 1–8: General purpose I/O
  • Port 2 (P2.0–P2.7) – Pins 21–28: I/O and High Address Bus
  • Port 3 (P3.0–P3.7) – Pins 10–17: I/O with alternate functions (UART, interrupts, timers)
  • XTAL1, XTAL2 – Pins 18,19: Crystal oscillator inputs
  • RST – Pin 9: Reset
  • VCC – Pin 40: +5V power
  • GND – Pin 20: Ground
  • EA – Pin 31: External Access (HIGH = use internal ROM)
  • ALE – Pin 30: Address Latch Enable
What is ALE (Address Latch Enable)?
ALE is a signal that tells an external latch IC to capture and hold the lower 8 bits of the address that are being sent on Port 0. Since Port 0 is multiplexed (shares address and data), ALE is HIGH when Port 0 carries the address, so a 74HC373 latch can capture it. Then Port 0 switches to carry data.

Interrupts in 8051

An interrupt is a signal that temporarily suspends the main program to handle an urgent event.

The 8051 has 5 interrupt sources organized in 2 priority levels:

What is an Instruction? An instruction is a binary command that tells the CPU exactly what operation to perform. For example, MOV A, #55H means "put the value 55 hexadecimal into the Accumulator register." Instructions are stored in program memory (ROM) and executed one by one. The 8051 instruction set includes ~111 instructions.

Arduino UNO

The Arduino UNO is the most popular beginner microcontroller board. It is based on the ATmega328p microcontroller.

An 8-bit microcontroller means its CPU processes 8 bits of data at a time. The ATmega328p is a RISC-based AVR microcontroller by Atmel/Microchip.

  • Executes most instructions in single clock cycle
  • 131 instructions in its instruction set
  • Has ADC, UART, SPI, I2C built-in
  • Watchdog timer, sleep modes for power saving

🍓 Raspberry Pi

Raspberry Pi is a low-cost, credit card–sized single-board computer (SBC) developed by the Raspberry Pi Foundation (UK). Unlike Arduino (a microcontroller), Raspberry Pi runs a full operating system (usually Raspberry Pi OS, a Linux-based system).

Raspberry Pi 4 Model B — Specifications

  • CPU: Broadcom BCM2711 (Quad-core 64-bit ARM Cortex-A72 @ 1.8 GHz)
  • RAM: 2GB / 4GB / 8GB LPDDR4
  • Storage: MicroSD card slot
  • GPU: VideoCore VI (4K video support)
  • Ports: 2× USB 3.0, 2× USB 2.0, 2× micro-HDMI
  • Connectivity: Gigabit Ethernet, WiFi, Bluetooth 5.0
  • GPIO: 40-pin header (General Purpose I/O)
  • Camera port (CSI), Display port (DSI)
  • Power: 5V/3A USB-C
  • OS: Raspberry Pi OS, Ubuntu, Windows IoT

What makes Raspberry Pi special?

  • Runs a full Linux OS → you can use Python, OpenCV, TensorFlow, ROS
  • Has HDMI output → connect to a monitor, use it as a desktop PC
  • Has camera module support → video processing, face recognition
  • 40-pin GPIO → can connect sensors, LEDs, motors (like Arduino)
  • Can run web servers, databases, complex AI models

Use in Robotics

  • Running ROS (Robot Operating System)
  • Image processing with OpenCV
  • Machine learning inference (TensorFlow Lite)
  • Controlling complex robots needing high-level computation
  • Web-based robot monitoring dashboards
Arduino vs Raspberry Pi: Arduino is best for real-time hardware control (reading sensors fast, PWM). Raspberry Pi is best for computation-heavy tasks (AI, video, networking). In advanced robots, BOTH are used together — Raspberry Pi for intelligence, Arduino for hardware I/O.

📶 ESP8266 — WiFi Microcontroller

The ESP8266 is a low-cost WiFi microcontroller chip developed by Espressif Systems. It can connect to WiFi networks and acts as both a microcontroller and a WiFi module — making it perfect for IoT (Internet of Things) projects.

ESP8266 Specifications

  • CPU: 32-bit Tensilica Xtensa LX106 @ 80/160 MHz
  • Flash Memory: 512KB – 4MB (varies)
  • RAM: 80KB user-accessible
  • WiFi: 802.11 b/g/n (2.4 GHz)
  • GPIO: 16 pins (varies by module)
  • 1 ADC pin (10-bit, 0-1V)
  • Communication: UART, SPI, I2C, PWM
  • Operating voltage: 3.3V
  • Programming: Arduino IDE, MicroPython, AT commands

Popular ESP8266 Modules

  • ESP-01 – Tiny module, only 2 GPIO pins, basic WiFi
  • NodeMCU – Development board with USB, 11 GPIO, popular for learning
  • Wemos D1 Mini – Compact development board

Applications

  • WiFi-controlled robots
  • Smart home automation (control lights via phone)
  • IoT sensor nodes (send temperature to cloud)
  • Web server on the microcontroller itself
  • MQTT-based communication for robot swarms

Robot Vision, Image Processing & AI

How robots see and understand the world around them.

🖼️ Digital Image & Pixel

A pixel (short for "Picture Element") is the smallest unit of a digital image. Every digital image is made up of a grid of pixels arranged in rows and columns.

What is a Pixel?

  • Each pixel stores color information
  • In a grayscale image: each pixel = 1 number (0=black, 255=white)
  • In a color (RGB) image: each pixel = 3 numbers (Red, Green, Blue values 0–255)
  • A 1920×1080 image has 1920×1080 = 2,073,600 pixels (≈2 megapixels)

Digital Image

A digital image is a 2D matrix of pixel values stored as numbers. Computers store and process images as arrays of numbers — this is how OpenCV and image processing libraries work.

🔬 Image Processing

Image processing is the technique of performing operations on a digital image to improve its quality, extract useful information, or transform it for further analysis. It's the foundation of computer vision and robot vision.

Image Processing Steps (Pipeline)

1

Image Acquisition

Capture image using a camera (webcam, Raspberry Pi camera, IP camera)

2

Pre-processing

Improve image quality — resize, denoise (Gaussian blur), adjust brightness/contrast

3

Color Space Conversion

Convert BGR → Grayscale, or BGR → HSV (HSV is better for color-based detection)

4

Segmentation

Separate objects from background using thresholding, color masking, or edge detection

5

Feature Extraction

Find edges, corners, blobs, contours, or use deep learning features

6

Analysis / Classification

Identify what objects are present, their position, size, shape using algorithms or AI

7

Output / Decision

Display result on screen, send command to robot actuators, store data

Canny Edge Detection

Canny edge detection is a popular multi-step algorithm for finding edges in images:

1

Gaussian Blur

Smooth the image to reduce noise (noise creates false edges)

2

Gradient Calculation

Find intensity change in X and Y directions using Sobel filters. Steep change = edge.

3

Non-Maximum Suppression

Thin thick edges to sharp 1-pixel-wide lines

4

Double Thresholding

Classify pixels as strong edge, weak edge, or not an edge using two threshold values

5

Edge Tracking (Hysteresis)

Keep weak edges only if connected to strong edges. Final clean edge map produced.

# Python OpenCV - Canny Edge Detection
import cv2

img = cv2.imread('robot.jpg')
gray = cv2.cvtColor(img, cv2.COLOR_BGR2GRAY)
blurred = cv2.GaussianBlur(gray, (5,5), 0)
edges = cv2.Canny(blurred, 50, 150) # threshold1=50, threshold2=150
cv2.imshow('Edges', edges)
cv2.waitKey(0)

🧠 Artificial Intelligence, Machine Learning & CNNs

Artificial Intelligence (AI)

AI is the simulation of human intelligence in machines. AI enables machines to learn from experience, understand natural language, recognize patterns, solve problems, and make decisions — tasks that typically require human intelligence.

Machine Learning (ML)

Machine Learning is a subset of AI. Instead of being explicitly programmed with rules, ML systems learn patterns from data and improve their performance over time without being explicitly programmed for each task.

Analogy: Traditional programming: Rules + Data → Output. Machine Learning: Data + Output → Rules (learned automatically).

Types of Machine Learning

  • Supervised Learning – Trained on labeled examples (input-output pairs)
  • Unsupervised Learning – Finds hidden patterns in unlabeled data
  • Reinforcement Learning – Learns by trial and error, rewarded for good actions

Supervised Learning in Detail

In supervised learning, the algorithm is given a training dataset where each input has a known, correct output (label).

1

Collect labeled data

2

Train the model

Feed data to algorithm. It learns the relationship between inputs and labels.

3

Evaluate

4

Deploy

Use trained model to predict labels for new unknown inputs.

ROS — Robot Operating System

The standard framework for building complex robot software.

🌐 What is ROS (Robot Operating System)?

ROS is an open-source robotics middleware framework (not a real operating system) that provides tools, libraries, and conventions for building complex robot software. It handles communication between different parts of a robot's software.

Why ROS? Without ROS, if you have a robot with 10 programs (camera, arm control, navigation, speech, etc.), you'd need to write complex code for them all to communicate. ROS provides a standard way for all these programs to talk to each other using a publish/subscribe messaging system.

Key Features of ROS

  • Hardware abstraction (works with different hardware)
  • Low-level device control
  • Message passing between processes
  • Package management
  • Ready-made libraries (navigation, SLAM, vision)
  • Visualization tools (RViz, rqt)
  • Simulation support (Gazebo)
  • Supported languages: Python, C++

ROS Architecture Overview

Figure: ROS nodes communicate via topics, registered with ROS Master

🔵 ROS Nodes

A ROS Node is an individual executable process that performs a specific task in a robot's software system. Nodes are the fundamental computational units in ROS.

Key Properties of a Node

  • Each node is a single program (Python or C++ file)
  • Nodes run independently and can be started/stopped separately
  • Nodes communicate by publishing/subscribing to topics
  • Each node registers with the ROS Master on startup
  • A typical robot has dozens of nodes running simultaneously

Example Nodes in a Robot

  • camera_node → captures images from camera
  • lidar_node → reads LIDAR distance data
  • navigation_node → plans path to destination
  • motor_controller_node → sends commands to motors
  • arm_node → controls robotic arm joints
  • speech_node → converts text to speech
# Simple ROS Publisher Node (Python)
import rospy
from std_msgs.msg import String

rospy.init_node('hello_node') # Register node with ROS Master
pub = rospy.Publisher('/greetings', String, queue_size=10)
rate = rospy.Rate(1) # 1 Hz = 1 message per second

while not rospy.is_shutdown():
  pub.publish("Hello from Robot!")
  rate.sleep()

📨 ROS Topics, Messages & Services

ROS Topics

A ROS Topic is a named communication channel through which nodes exchange data. Topics follow a publish-subscribe model:

  • Publisher Node – Writes data to a topic
  • Subscriber Node – Reads data from that topic
  • Multiple publishers and multiple subscribers can use the same topic
  • Topics are one-way (from publisher to subscribers) and asynchronous
Analogy: Think of a topic like a YouTube channel. A creator (publisher) posts videos. Many viewers (subscribers) watch the channel. They don't interact directly — it's one-way, and the creator doesn't know who's watching.

ROS Messages

A ROS Message is the data format used to communicate on topics. Each topic has a fixed message type. Messages are defined in .msg files and can contain basic types (int, float, string, bool) or other messages.

Unlike topics (one-way), ROS Services implement a request-response communication model — like a function call. A client sends a request and waits for a response from the server node.

🌍 Gazebo Robot Simulator

Gazebo is a powerful open-source 3D robot simulation environment that integrates seamlessly with ROS. It allows developers to test and develop robot software in a realistic virtual environment without needing physical hardware.

Key Features of Gazebo

  • Physics Engine – Realistic simulation of gravity, friction, collisions (ODE, Bullet physics)
  • Sensor Simulation – Virtual cameras, LIDAR, IMU, GPS all produce realistic data
  • 3D Environment – Build indoor rooms, outdoor terrains, obstacle courses
  • ROS Integration – Runs like a real robot — same ROS nodes and topics work
  • Multiple Robots – Simulate robot swarms
  • Plugin System – Add custom sensors, controllers, worlds
  • SDF/URDF Support – Define robot models in XML format
  • Cloud Simulation – Run on cloud servers for testing

Applications of Gazebo

  • Algorithm Development – Test navigation, SLAM, manipulation algorithms safely
  • Hardware-in-Loop Testing – Simulate hardware before it's built
  • Machine Learning Training – Train RL agents in simulation (sim-to-real transfer)
  • Education – Students learn robot programming without expensive hardware
  • Competitive Robotics – RoboCup, DARPA robotics challenges use Gazebo for testing
Example: NASA uses Gazebo to test Mars rover software before deployment. Self-driving car companies train their algorithms in Gazebo-like simulators with millions of simulated miles before testing on real roads.

Automation in Robotics

Fixed, programmable automation and their role in modern industry.

⚙️ What is Automation?

Automation is the use of technology to perform tasks with minimal or no human intervention. In robotics and manufacturing, automation means using machines, robots, and computer systems to carry out repetitive or complex tasks automatically.

🔧

Fixed Automation

Hardwired for one specific task. Cannot be reprogrammed easily.

💾

Programmable Automation

Can be reprogrammed for different tasks and products.

🤖

Flexible Automation

Quickly adapts to different products with minimal changeover time.

Benefits of Automation

  • Increased production speed and throughput
  • Higher consistency and quality (no human error)
  • Reduced labor costs for repetitive tasks
  • Ability to work 24/7 without breaks
  • Safer operation in hazardous environments

🔒 Fixed Automation

Fixed automation (also called hard automation) uses equipment with a fixed configuration designed to perform a single, specific operation or sequence of operations. The sequence of steps is fixed in the hardware — it cannot be changed without physically rebuilding the machine.

Characteristics

  • Designed for one specific product or task
  • Very high production rate
  • Very high initial investment cost
  • Not flexible — difficult to reconfigure for new products
  • Economical only for very high-volume production

Examples of Fixed Automation

  • Automated conveyor systems in bottling plants (filling, capping, labeling in fixed sequence)
  • Transfer machines in engine manufacturing (machining operations in fixed sequence)
  • Automatic car wash machines (fixed sequence of wash, rinse, dry)
  • Chocolate manufacturing line (fixed sequence: mold, fill, cool, package)
  • Dial index machines (circular conveyor with fixed machining stations)
When to use Fixed Automation: When you need to make millions of identical products. The high initial cost is justified by the extremely high production rate and low per-unit cost.
